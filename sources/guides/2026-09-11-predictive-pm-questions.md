# Predictive Project Management PMP Questions

*Yassine Tounsi — PMP 35 Hours Master Course*

**Q1.** A project team has developed the initial schedule using a network diagramming method and has
arrived at the overall project duration. Upon examining the schedule, the PM finds that the duration
of several activities requires adjustment to address potential resource constraints. Match the
following common resource issues with their explanations.
**Answer:**
- **Dropped Baton:** An activity is not ready to start, and the preceding activity finishes early,
  resulting in lost time between activities.
- **Student Syndrome:** Procrastination in starting activities can use up valuable slack built into the
  activity duration.
- **Self-protection:** Workers are reluctant to finish tasks early so as not to communicate their
  efficiency and set precedents for future tasks.
- **Parkinson's Law:** Workers use all the time allocated for an activity regardless of how long it
  actually takes to complete the task.
- **Sandbagging:** Workers intentionally underestimate or lower expectations to produce relatively
  greater-than-anticipated results.

**Q2.** In the project initiation phase, the sponsor asked the PM to provide an approximate cost
estimate for the project by the end of the day. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Provide an estimate based on similar past projects.
**Explanation:** Since the sponsor requested the cost estimate on short notice, early in the project
with little to no information available for cost estimation, the most practical method is to rely on
similar past projects' data — analogous estimating, based on the known costs of a similarly completed
project. Along with historical data, this technique relies on the PM's expert judgment. (Building a
product backlog and estimating iterations, creating the WBS and summing work-package costs, or
creating the cost management plan are all too involved for a same-day, early-stage estimate.)

**Q3.** A PM is closely monitoring the performance of an ongoing project. Over four months, SPI trends
from 1.00 up to about 1.25 (increasingly ahead of schedule), while CPI trends from 1.00 down to about
0.70 (increasingly over budget). What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Take corrective actions to get the cost back on track, considering that the project is
experiencing cost overruns and is ahead of schedule.
**Explanation:** CPI < 1 means over budget; SPI > 1 means ahead of schedule. Since schedule is healthy
(even ahead) but cost is deteriorating, the corrective action belongs on cost, not schedule — treating
both as broken, or doing nothing because schedule looks fine, both misread the data.

**Q4.** A PM is leading a construction project following a traditional waterfall approach. The project
team has gathered requirements from the customer, who is eager to kick off the project promptly. The
PM meets with the business analyst to develop the scope statement based on the gathered requirements.
What should the PM do next?
**Answer:** Develop the scope description, deliverables, acceptance criteria, and exclusions.
**Explanation:** The project scope statement includes the scope description, deliverables, acceptance
criteria, and exclusions (PMBOK 7th edition, page 246) — the WBS/WBS dictionary, the Scope Management
Plan/SOW/RTM, and the project charter/PM plan/deliverables are all separate documents that come before
or after the scope statement itself.

**Q5.** Team members have been allocated to a project with 50% availability. Two months into execution,
the PM discovers the team has been spending less than 50% of their time on the project, and the
schedule is about to become heavily delayed. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Negotiate clear and written assignments with reliable scheduling priorities with
functional managers.
**Explanation:** Since the PM didn't assign the team members personally, the organization must be
functional — staff assignments are the functional managers' responsibility, not the project sponsor's.
Requesting more resources from the sponsor, adjusting the schedule/deadlines, or crashing the project
all skip the actual root cause (competing priorities set by functional managers).

**Q6.** A procurement manager and a PM meet to assess the final performance of the project's primary
vendor — discussing issues like late delivery and budget overruns, and analyzing what could have been
done differently and what went well. What activity are they engaged in?
**Answer:** Capturing lessons learned.
**Explanation:** Identifying what went well and what could have been done differently with this vendor
will benefit future projects — this is lessons learned, not an open claim, a work performance report,
or formal acceptance of the vendor's work.

**Q7.** The team of a predictive project discovers that many implemented features are different from
the outlined scope in the scope statement. Which document should the PM refer to in order to deal with
this matter?
**Answer:** Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM).
**Explanation:** The RTM is a document that maps requirements and other aspects of the project — used
as evidence to confirm requirements have been fulfilled, typically documenting them along with issues
and test results (PMBOK 7th edition, page 189). Flow: Requirements → Scope statement → WBS/WBS
Dictionary → Execution.

**Q8.** A PM is planning a cryptocurrency conference. The sponsor (Ministry of Finance) limited
attendees to forty. One day before the event, the ministry requests adding two more attendees — the
room can fit fifty, but lodging, catering, coffee breaks, and gift packages all need rearranging. What
should the PM do first?
**Answer:** Issue a change request to address the ministry's request.
**Explanation:** When a critical key stakeholder requests a change, the PM should treat it with high
priority and issue a change request to the Change Control Board (CCB) in order to add their request to
the scope (PMBOK 7th edition, page 77) — accepting or refusing the request outright both skip the
formal change process, even for a sponsor-originated request.

**Q9.** A project's current SPI is 1.10 (ahead of schedule) and CPI is 0.80 (over budget). What should
the PM do to balance out schedule and budget performance?
**Answer:** Level out resource usage to further spread out costs over time.
**Explanation:** The project is 10% ahead of schedule while over budget. Leveling out resource usage
spreads costs over time, reducing unexpected spending spikes, though it may lengthen the schedule —
which is acceptable here given the schedule cushion. Crashing or fast-tracking would only make the
budget problem worse, and "do nothing" ignores the cost overrun.

**Q10.** During execution, a PM needed a scope deviation and submitted a plan to the CCB. The CCB
approved the change but with a different implementation method than the PM wanted. What should the PM
do?
**Answer:** Implement the change using the approved method.
**Explanation:** The CCB's decision should be respected, following the integrated change control
process, without taking it personally — resigning or quitting would be unprofessional, and building a
prototype with the rejected method is scope creep (work neither requested by the client nor included
in the scope statement).

**Q11.** A PM leading a political-party marketing project verbally agreed by phone to an $8,000
addition (social media ads) with the client representative. When invoiced, the representative refused
to pay, claiming an unacceptable project delay should have been penalized instead. What should the PM
have done to avoid this? (Select two)
**Answer:** Used a more appropriate (formal) communication channel, and requested a schedule extension
to accommodate the additional work.
**Explanation:** This could have been avoided with formal communication when discussing/approving
contract-term changes, and by considering the change's impact on scope, time, and resources — not just
cost, which is all the PM analyzed on that phone call.

**Q12.** A couple planning a tiny-house project disagreed on interior design details, but wanted the
house done by year-end, so they started construction first and decided interior design details later.
What technique are they using?
**Answer:** Rolling Wave planning.
**Explanation:** Rolling Wave addresses uncertainty by planning near-term work in detail while planning
future work at a higher level (PMBOK 7th edition, page 249) — a form of progressive elaboration that
lets current/near-term deliverables proceed while planning for later work continues.

**Q13.** A sponsor submitted a change request to a 5-member CCB (of which the PM is a member), and the
board's opinions diverged on whether to accept or reject it. What's the best way to reach a decision?
**Answer:** Try to reach a consensus.
**Explanation:** Collaboration is the best approach to resolving conflict between members — open
dialogue where differing viewpoints are discussed to reach consensus and commit to the final decision
(PMBOK 7th edition, page 168), rather than voting, deferring to the most experienced member, or
deferring to the board director.

**Q14.** After completing all requirements, a PM and product manager review each item of the
deliverable acceptance criteria; 2 of 30 deliverables don't meet requirements, so the product manager
asks for fixes before signing off the final delivery document. Which process are they performing?
**Answer:** Validate scope.
**Explanation:** Validate Scope compares deliverables against acceptance criteria/requirements to
formally sign off completed deliverables — Control Quality and Control Scope happen before Validate
Scope, not during it.

**Q15.** A PM on a two-phase amusement park project (phase 1: import attractions; phase 2: install
everything) just finished phase 1. What should the PM do next?
**Answer:** Identify stakeholders.
**Explanation:** Identify Stakeholders should happen before or concurrently with creating/approving the
project charter, and should be repeated as needed — always at the start of each project phase, and
whenever a significant change occurs at the project or organization level.

**Q16.** After executing a rebranding project, several key stakeholders (including the sponsor) are
reluctant to accept deliverables and close the project. What should the PM do? (Select three)
**Answer:** Identify and resolve any open issues, then be firm on formal closure; identify and openly
discuss the reasons for reluctance; invite earnest feedback from all sides to identify any
misunderstanding.
**Explanation:** The PM should not close the project while key stakeholders are unsatisfied — discuss,
identify, and resolve issues/misunderstandings first, rather than closing regardless and assuming
stakeholders will "get acquainted" with the new brand over time.

**Q17.** During execution, a PM found tasks weren't being performed in the right order at the right
time, causing rework and bad morale. Which tool should the PM implement?
**Answer:** Work authorization system.
**Explanation:** A work authorization system is a set of formal documented procedures establishing how
project work will be authorized, ensuring work happens at the right time and in the correct sequence —
distinct from an org chart, a RACI matrix, or the communications management plan.

**Q18.** A project meant to last 6 months is currently in its second year. Which statement best
describes the situation?
**Answer:** The project scope was not clearly defined.
**Explanation:** Most likely the extended duration traces back to an unclear scope. There's no
indication the project should have been split into sub-projects; the project team doesn't have the
authority to approve/reject change requests (that's the CCB's job); and since the sponsor is still
supporting the project past its original deadline, halting it isn't indicated either.

**Q19.** A PM is reviewing a change request to integrate a third-party system the team hasn't worked
with before; the team says implementation will take no more than two months *if* the system follows
the international standard. What should the PM do after the change request is approved?
**Answer:** Update the assumption log noting the basis of the two-month estimation.
**Explanation:** An assumption is a factor considered true/real/certain without proof; the assumption
log records all assumptions and constraints throughout the project (PMBOK 7th edition, page 185) — the
next step is documenting the team's assumption that the system follows the international standard, not
checking critical-path impact, running a parametric estimate, or holding a meeting to re-evaluate the
estimation approach.

**Q20.** A sponsor informs the PM the project must finish one week sooner than originally agreed. What
should the PM do first?
**Answer:** Fast-track the schedule.
**Explanation:** Between the two schedule compression techniques, crashing (add resources → costs more)
and fast-tracking (run originally sequential activities in parallel instead) (PMBOK 7th edition, page
59), pick the least costly option first when possible — that's fast-tracking, not crashing, adding
duration buffers, or leveling resources (which wouldn't shorten the schedule at all).
