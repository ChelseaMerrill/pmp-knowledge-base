Monte Carlo simulations in risk management are a statistical technique used to predict the range of
possible outcomes for a project by running thousands (or millions) of "what-if" scenarios based on
uncertainties in the inputs.

Instead of giving a single point estimate (e.g. "the project will take 12 months"), Monte Carlo produces
a **probability distribution** showing, for example, there's a 70% chance you'll finish within 11 months,
and a 90% chance within 14 months.

> *Figure in the original handout:* a Monte Carlo output chart showing a project having a 90% probability
> of finishing by October.

## 1. How it works

1. **Define uncertain variables.** Identify which parts of your project are uncertain and could vary in
   outcome. These might include the duration of certain activities, the cost of raw materials, or labor
   productivity rates.

2. **Assign probability distributions.** Describe each uncertain variable using a probability
   distribution. For example, you might estimate that foundation work could take a minimum of 8 days, a
   most likely duration of 10 days, and a maximum of 14 days.

3. **Run simulations.** Once the distributions are set, simulation software repeatedly selects different
   values from each distribution and calculates the total project outcome for that combination. This
   process is repeated thousands of times to explore the full range of possibilities, producing a large
   set of potential project scenarios.

4. **Analyze results.** The results are compiled into probability curves and histograms, giving a clear
   picture of likely outcomes. From this, you can determine the probability of meeting a deadline or
   staying within budget, and decide what level of contingency is needed to achieve a desired confidence
   level, such as 80% or 99%.

## 2. Benefits of using Monte Carlo simulations

- Handles uncertainty and variability better than simple estimates.
- Quantifies risk exposure in time and cost.
- Helps in contingency planning.
- Supports data-driven decision-making with probabilities rather than guesses.
