# Project Team Management PMP Questions

*Yassine Tounsi — PMP 35 Hours Master Course*

**Q1.** Leading the development of a new mobile banking application, the PM and a team member
responsible for a critical task have conflicting perspectives on a key deliverable — the team member
feels micromanaged, while the PM believes the work is subpar. The sponsor unexpectedly emails the PM:
the team member has asked for her intervention. What should the PM do next?
**Answer:** Sit down with both the team member and the sponsor to collaboratively resolve the issue.
**Explanation:** This is a proactive approach — facilitating open dialogue and seeking mutual
understanding works toward a resolution that satisfies both parties and aligns with project
objectives. (Removing the team member, escalating straight to the functional manager for discipline,
or convening a full stakeholder conflict-resolution meeting all skip past the direct, collaborative
conversation.)

**Q2.** In an Agile mobile-app project, team members rotate facilitating daily stand-ups. One team
member consistently fails to facilitate well — flustered, not engaging others, doing most of the
talking. What should the PM do next?
**Answer:** Organize coaching sessions with the team member to discuss the importance of their role
and provide guidance on how to improve.
**Explanation:** Coaching gives the PM a way to provide support, guidance, and feedback to help the
team member overcome the hardships they're having as a facilitator — reassigning the role, publicly
critiquing them after each stand-up, or removing them from the team all skip the development
opportunity.

**Q3.** A team member with deep Agile expertise often mentors new team members, but during
retrospectives and planning meetings assertively voices his opinions without fully considering
alternative perspectives. Which two MBTI traits describe this team member's personality?
**Answer:** Extrovert and Judging.
**Explanation:** Volunteering to actively engage in coaching/mentoring indicates an extroverted trait.
Being assertive and decisive in expressing opinions without fully considering alternatives aligns with
the Judging trait.

**Q4.** A PM is leading an experienced marketing team (who have worked together before) on a new ad
campaign. At which stage of Tuckman's ladder should the team charter be created?
**Answer:** At the Forming stage, to set the foundation for how the team will work together throughout
the project.
**Explanation:** Creating the team charter during Forming lets the PM establish clear goals, roles,
and expectations from the outset — a solid foundation for effective collaboration and communication as
the team progresses through Storming, Norming, Performing, and Adjourning.

**Q5.** A PM leads a hybrid project with remote team members across four continents, and the team has
communication issues. What can the PM do to help? (Select two)
**Answer:** Set up remote pairing, and create fishbowl windows.
**Explanation:** Both are techniques for managing communication in remote teams. Fishbowl windows are
long-lived video conferences team members join at the start of the workday and leave at the end.
Remote pairing is when two or more people join an online event at a specific time to discuss and share
screens as needed (Agile Practice Guide, p. 46). Colocating a globally distributed team, or simply
"using an agile approach," don't address the actual communication mechanics.

**Q6.** On a construction project, work on light spots is pending because ceiling tiles are still
being installed, which may cause delays. What should the PM check to see the electricity team's
availability over the next two weeks to resolve this dependency?
**Answer:** Resource Calendars.
**Explanation:** A resource calendar plans, manages, and monitors resources (employees and equipment),
giving the PM an overview of how resources are being utilized, which are available, and when — RAM,
RACI, and Organigram describe responsibility/reporting structure, not availability.

**Q7.** The project team's morale is low after the departure of a key stakeholder who had been a big
support and motivation source. What should the PM do first?
**Answer:** Understand the team's needs and what motivates them.
**Explanation:** The PM should first identify each team member's incentives — this reveals what
conditions they need to reach their potential and stay encouraged long-term. In most cases the PM
can't prevent a stakeholder's departure once decided, and setting objectives or holding team-building
activities won't necessarily replace whatever motivational factor (e.g. recognition) the departed
stakeholder was providing.

**Q8.** On a multi-year software project, a senior team member starts losing motivation, complaining
their assigned work could easily be done by a junior. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Find more challenging tasks for the senior team member to tackle.
**Explanation:** The demotivation is intrinsic — the senior member wants challenging work and will
respond well to stretch goals and problem-solving (PMBOK 7th edition, p. 25). A raise or team-building
activities don't address the real cause; looking for a replacement should only be a last resort if all
other options fail.

**Q9.** While the PM is on vacation, a team member volunteers to deliver the monthly status
presentation. Stakeholders later give feedback that the presentation was not good. What should the PM
do?
**Answer:** Ask the team member what happened with the presentation that led to the stakeholders'
dissatisfaction.
**Explanation:** The best approach is an informal discussion to understand what happened and provide
appropriate guidance — not disciplinary emails demanding explanation, blanket advice to never
volunteer again, or ignoring the issue.

**Q10.** After an org-wide transition to Agile, the PM expected the team to become self-organized, but
team members still wait for the PM to assign them work. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Mentor the team on how to make their own decisions.
**Explanation:** This empowers the team to pick their own tasks without waiting for assignments or
permission — ensuring diverse functional expertise or removing impediments doesn't address the root
cause (the team hasn't learned to self-organize), and taking time off to force the issue is not a
deliberate approach.

**Q11.** In the last three daily stand-ups, the PM notices a new team member making no progress on an
assigned task. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Check whether other team members can help their new colleague with the task after the
stand-up.
**Explanation:** Since the member is new and may lack certain skills or experience, pairing them with
more experienced team members lets them learn and overcome the impediment — reassigning the task away
from them, expressing disappointment, or just pointing them to the ground rules don't build that
capability.

**Q12.** Four months into execution, some team members' performance is deteriorating while others'
stays strong. What should the PM do to get the whole team back on track?
**Answer:** Identify the causes of bad performance, solicit systematic feedback, and implement
adequate solutions based on findings.
**Explanation:** The PM's objective is to understand why a once-competent team member is now
struggling — recognize the symptoms, reach out, talk to find the cause, offer help, monitor/measure
progress, and share feedback. Publicly discussing individuals' poor performance with the whole team,
setting up a competitive bonus system, or simply not interfering all skip this diagnostic step.

**Q13.** A team with members distributed across countries seems disconnected (on top of cultural
barriers), leading to rework. What could the PM do to improve communication?
**Answer:** Hold short daily meetings with all team members to sync on what everyone is doing.
**Explanation:** Daily stand-ups build connection, improve communication, and reduce rework as a
result. Co-location isn't feasible with team members spread across countries; email-only communication
increases misinterpretation risk; cultural-difference training alone doesn't help the team sync its
work.

**Q14.** Before a client demo, a team member finds a defective component that could be repaired or
replaced — both options are negligible in cost, risk-free, and take under an hour, but the team spends
15 minutes debating without agreement. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Call for a vote in order to reach a decision.
**Explanation:** Voting reaches a consensus decision efficiently by selecting whichever option gets
more votes — simple, intuitive, time- and cost-efficient. Given the negligible cost/risk/duration of
either option, it's not worth letting the discussion continue, setting up a formal meeting, or
escalating to the sponsor.

**Q15.** In a marketing campaign project, two team members disagree on target audience and messaging —
one wants younger demographics with trendy messaging, the other wants older demographics with
traditional messaging. What should the PM do next?
**Answer:** Conduct market research and analysis to gather data on target audience preferences and
behavior, to inform decision-making and resolve the conflict.
**Explanation:** Objective data resolves conflicting opinions based on evidence, ensuring the target
audience and messaging strategy are grounded in market insight and aligned with project objectives —
delegating to an outside agency, rotating decision-making between the two, or a simple team vote don't
ground the decision in evidence.

**Q16.** A PM decides Kanban is the ideal Agile framework for a new project, but the team is
unfamiliar with it. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Assign training sessions for the team to learn about Kanban in practice.
**Explanation:** If the PM believes Kanban is the best fit, the team needs the training to execute it
properly — of the options, setting up training sessions is most consistent with servant leadership and
agile best practices, giving the team the knowledge/mastery to carry out the project. (Refraining from
using it, briefly explaining it in one meeting, or leaving the team to self-teach all fall short.)

**Q17.** A PM sits with a frustrated key team member, who explains a coworker's mistakes made him fall
behind schedule. The PM reassures him that she's aware of the situation and that his hard work is
noticed and appreciated. What conflict resolution technique did the PM use?
**Answer:** Smoothing.
**Explanation:** Smoothing gives more consideration to one side of a conflict to smooth the situation
and avoid intensifying it — here the PM downplays the seriousness of the issue by reassuring the team
member, which often establishes harmony and prevents amplifying non-critical issues.

**Q18.** A PM starting a two-year project with team members from different locations/cultures wants to
set up a reward strategy after attending a motivation webinar. Which two reward strategies fit best?
**Answer:** Set a reward for the team every 6 months, and consider cultural differences when
determining rewards.
**Explanation:** PMs should recognize team members throughout the project life cycle, not just at the
end — and cultural differences should be factored in, since recognition and rewards are perceived
differently across cultures.

**Q19.** A PM sets daily stand-up meeting times for a project with remote team members, but not
everyone finds the time convenient due to time zone differences. Which conflict resolution technique
fits?
**Answer:** Compromise.
**Explanation:** Since the time zone difference is beyond anyone's control, a compromise brings some
degree of satisfaction to all parties — perceived as the best outcome even if no one is fully happy
with the final resolution (PMBOK 7th edition, p. 168).

**Q20.** A PM takes over a climate-smart agriculture project after the previous PM quit. At the start
of execution, team members have differing opinions on project work, deliverables, and overall
complexity. What should the PM do?
**Answer:** Organize a meeting involving all team members to identify and resolve misunderstandings,
in order to avoid issues, disintegration, and rework.
**Explanation:** Problem-solving/collaboration techniques are the best course of action here — a
meeting to identify and resolve misunderstandings among team members, rather than not intervening,
treating this purely as a risk to log, or having one-on-one conversations that don't bring the
differing opinions into the same room.
