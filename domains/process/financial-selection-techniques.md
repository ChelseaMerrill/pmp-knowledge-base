# Financial Project-Selection Techniques

Covers the ECO Process task "plan and manage finance" and the broader question of how organizations
decide whether a project is worth doing. Distinct from EVM (which measures a project already in
flight) — these are typically used before or across projects, to compare and justify them.

## The core metrics

| Metric | What it tells you | Rule of thumb |
|---|---|---|
| **Benefit-Cost Ratio (BCR)** | Benefits ÷ Costs | BCR > 1 → benefits outweigh costs (e.g. BCR of 1.3 means benefits/revenue are 1.3× the cost). BCR < 1 → costs outweigh benefits. |
| **Cost-Benefit Analysis** | The financial method that produces the BCR — weighs a project's benefits against its costs. | Used to make the case for a solution to stakeholders. |
| **Net Present Value (NPV)** | Whether a project's anticipated financial gains outweigh its present-day investment, in today's dollars. | Positive NPV → expected to be profitable. Negative NPV → expected loss. |
| **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** | The discount rate at which a project's NPV would be zero. | When comparing projects, the higher IRR is generally the better choice. |
| **Payback Period** | How long it takes for a project's revenue to break even against its total investment. | After the payback period, the project starts generating profit. Shorter is generally more attractive. |

## Sunk cost

Money already spent and unrecoverable. A sunk cost is irrelevant to future decisions — the exam
regularly tests that you shouldn't let sunk cost influence what to do next; decide based on remaining
costs/benefits only.

## How these fit together

A make-or-buy analysis, a business case, and a project charter can all lean on these metrics: the
business case uses cost-benefit analysis (and often NPV/IRR/payback) to justify the project before
it's authorized; once running, the make-or-buy analysis applies the same logic to individual sourcing
decisions.
