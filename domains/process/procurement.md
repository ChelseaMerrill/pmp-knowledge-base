# Procurement Management

Covers "plan and manage procurement." See the seeded glossary terms for the three core contract types
(Fixed-Price, Cost-Reimbursable, T&M), the Procurement Statement of Work, Make-or-Buy Analysis, and
Source Selection Criteria — this note adds the surrounding process and documents.

## The Procurement Management Plan

Part of the overall Project Management Plan (in a predictive/waterfall context) — sets the approach,
processes, guidelines, tools, and templates used for procurement on the project.

## Sourcing documents and events

- **RFQ (Request For Quotation):** ask for pricing on a well-defined item.
- **RFP (Request For Proposal):** ask for a full proposed approach, typically for less-defined,
  complex work.
- **RFI (Request For Information):** ask for general information before deciding what to formally
  request.
- **Invitation for Bid (IFB), Request for Tender, Invitation to Tender:** a document inviting suppliers/
  contractors to bid on a project or service — the "please submit a bid" counterpart to RFQ/RFP/RFI.
- **SoW (Statement of Work):** describes how the work will be performed and under what conditions.
- **Bidder conference (contractor/vendor/pre-bid conference):** the buyer answers seller questions and
  clarifies bid terms/conditions to all bidders at once, ensuring a level playing field (PMBOK 7th
  edition, page 70). It does exactly two things — clarify doubts and explain bid terms/conditions — it
  does **not** shortlist sellers or award the contract; those happen later, in separate steps.
- **Document responsibility split:** the **buyer** (PM) issues source selection criteria, the RFQ/RFP/
  RFI/IFB, and the Purchase Order. The **seller** prepares the **proposal** (or bid/quote) in response.
  On an exam question asking which documents "the PM is responsible for issuing," a proposal is the one
  that *isn't* — it's the seller's document.
- **Tendering methods:** Open tendering (public announcement, anyone can bid) vs. Selective/Restricted
  tendering (only personally-invited vendors can bid) vs. Negotiated tendering (buyer approaches a single
  supplier directly) vs. Single-stage bidding (one round, then evaluate and award) vs. Double-stage
  bidding/Two-stage tendering (initial technical-approach proposals, then detailed bids from the
  shortlist) vs. E-tendering/eProcurement (online platforms automate solicitation and evaluation).
- **Procurement strategy:** the macro-level approach to securing the physical resources the project
  needs — set before sourcing documents go out. **Market analysis/market survey** (what's available in
  the marketplace) and a **feasibility study** (is the proposed approach practical/viable) typically
  inform that strategy. **Bills of materials** lists the physical elements required for a deliverable or
  the whole project.

## Vendor selection and award

1. **Screening** — filter out sellers who don't meet minimum requirements. **Exit criteria** are exactly
   this filter from the seller's side: the conditions a seller's proposal must meet just to be
   *considered* at all — fail them, and the proposal is rejected outright before scoring even starts.
   (Don't confuse this procurement "exit criteria" with a project/phase's closure or kill-point
   criteria — see the glossary's Kill Point entry for that distinct, governance-level concept.)
2. **Weighting/Scoring** — filter further by qualitative evaluation criteria (source selection
   criteria, aka evaluation criteria) — a **weighting system/scoring model**. For a bid or quote, price
   is usually the main criterion; for a fuller proposal, criteria can include experience, references,
   and certifications too.
3. **Awarding** — the contract goes to the vendor with the highest score. For a **complex, high-value,
   high-risk** procurement, final approval before award generally also requires **senior/organizational
   management sign-off** (Process Groups: A Practice Guide (2022), page 238) — on top of, not instead of,
   the team's own selection-criteria scoring.

If proposals have already come in, **make-or-buy analysis** is already behind you — that decision (build
it ourselves vs. buy it) happens *before* soliciting proposals, not when comparing the ones you received.
A **procurement audit** or **performance review** happens later still, after the work is underway or
done, to verify the process was followed and the purchased items conform to requirements — not as a
same-stage substitute for selection criteria.

## Other procurement documents

Letter of Intent, Purchase Order (PO), Memorandum of Understanding (MOU), Service Level Agreement
(SLA), Memorandum of Agreement (MOA), Basic Ordering Agreement (BOA), and the Contract itself. Also:
**Notice to Proceed** (formal go-ahead to the supplier to start work), **Notice of Change** (formal
written notice of a modification), and **Notice of Termination** (written notice the contract is ending,
for convenience or default).

## Contract type subfamilies

- **Fixed-Price:** FFP (Firm Fixed Price), FPIF (Fixed Price Incentive Fee), FPEPA (Fixed Price with
  Economic Price Adjustment). Also in this family: **Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP)** — caps a
  construction project's price, with the contractor absorbing any cost beyond that cap — and **Unit
  Price Contract** — the seller quotes per-unit rates, and the final total price depends on how many
  units the work actually needs.
- **Cost-Reimbursable:** CPFF (Cost Plus Fixed Fee), CPAF (Cost Plus Award Fee), CPIF (Cost Plus
  Incentive Fee).
- **Time & Material:** typically used for freelancers, experts, and consultants — a hybrid of the
  above two families; sets a fixed rate but not a precise statement of work.

A contract with one clear total price for defined work (e.g. "$715,500 for an 18-month building
project") is Fixed-Price — the exam tell is a single agreed total, not a rate.

## Claims and disputes

**Escalation chain:** an unmanaged **risk** can become a **conflict**; an unaddressed conflict can
escalate into a **claim** (a formal request/demand tied to contractual obligations, payment, or
performance); an unresolved claim can become a full **dispute** requiring formal resolution.

**Common causes of claims and disputes:** failure to understand/comply with a contractual obligation;
errors or omissions in the contract document; an unrealistic contract duration or completion date.

**Claims management process** (four overlapping stages, spanning the procurement lifecycle from
Discovery through Contract Closeout):

1. **Claim Prevention** — identifying risks early and preventing them from escalating into claims
   (Discovery/Pre-award stages).
2. **Claim Mitigation** — good documentation, a change-order process, and a **Dispute Resolution Board
   (DRB)** set up in advance (spans Pre-award through Contract Execution).
3. **Pursuing Claims** — claim identification (proper documentation) and claim quantification
   (determining the additional compensation or time extension sought) — runs through Contract Execution
   and Closeout.
4. **Claim Resolution** — finding a way to resolve the claim and/or close the contract — also runs
   through Contract Execution and Closeout.

**Dispute resolution ladder**, least to most formal/costly: **Negotiation → Mediation → Adjudication →
Expert determination → Arbitration → Litigation.** Negotiation should always be tried first, and
face-to-face is more effective than email or a phone call. If direct negotiation fails, move to
Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) — mediation, arbitration, etc. — which sits above negotiation and
below litigation on this ladder; litigation is the last resort. A **claims resolution guideline** should
be set up front, during contract elaboration — not invented reactively once a dispute is already
underway.

## Key contract clauses

- **Payment clause:** the payment process (timeframes, conditions, required documents, approval process)
  and the final payment terms.
- **Change clause:** what can be changed, the change-order process, and how a new price gets established.
- **Indemnity clause:** transfers risk — indemnifies a party against loss or damage, and defines the
  triggering events/process.
- **Liquidated damages:** a pre-defined amount payable for damages (e.g. delays) — a substitute for
  having to prove actual damages in court.
- **Dispute resolution clause:** how to resolve claims without litigation — the ADR process and when a
  dispute escalates to court. This is the clause to check for the termination and ADR mechanism when
  ending a difficult supplier relationship.
- **Time-barred clause:** sets a deadline for claims — past that deadline, the contractor's claims are no
  longer admissible.
- **Termination clause:** defines when and how the contract can be ended.

## Monitoring and closing

- **Inspection** reviews the seller's delivery/performance; **statistical sampling** is one inspection
  technique (checking a representative subset rather than everything).
- A **procurement audit** formally reviews procurement processes, procedures, and activities across
  the project or organization.
- **Negotiation** matters both at contract award and when disputes arise — a win-win outcome is
  favored over a win-lose one.
- **Closing the contract** happens once all deliverables meet requirements and the closure is done
  administratively correctly (documentation, formal sign-off) — not just when the work "feels" done.
  Keep **both** the contract and the project open until a still-pending contract activity (e.g. a final
  seller inspection) is actually complete, even under time pressure to close the project — don't skip
  the inspection, and don't close one without the other.
- A **vendor-reported delay** shouldn't trigger an immediate reaction (renegotiating, replacing the
  vendor, updating the schedule) — first **evaluate the delay's actual impact** (is the activity on the
  critical path? does it exceed available lag?) before deciding whether any action is needed at all.
- **Contract vs. process documents:** when a vendor claims partial completion and requests payment, the
  document that settles it is the **contract** itself (the payment terms/schedule) — not the RFP, the
  seller's bid response (which may have been renegotiated before signing), or the procurement management
  plan (which describes *process*, not payment terms). Likewise, when a client dispute is about whether
  a deliverable is in scope, review the **contract's terms and conditions** first, before jumping to a
  change request or a stakeholder-engagement-plan review.
- A **sponsor-directed termination** (for any reason, including one external to the project like a
  changed site) should simply be executed — start the close-project process — rather than negotiated
  against or treated as a claims/dispute matter, since no contract dispute is actually in play.
- Even a **swap of physical resources** (e.g. substituting an unavailable raw material for an equivalent
  one) needs to go through the CCB as a change request before use — see
  [schedule-and-change-control.md](schedule-and-change-control.md) for the full integrated change control
  process. Only after approval does the resource management plan get updated to reflect the new material.

## Exam heuristics

- For a team performance issue, hiring an external resource is generally not the favored fix (see
  [leadership-and-team.md](leadership-and-team.md)).
- Generally avoid answers that raise cost — awards beyond scope, hiring externals, crashing, asking
  for more budget — unless the scenario genuinely calls for it.
- When a scenario names a specific *approved* change (e.g. "replace the defective part"), execute it
  exactly as approved — don't substitute your own judgment (e.g. repairing instead of replacing).
