# Leadership and Team Management

Covers "lead the project team" and "develop a common vision" — the styles, sources of authority, and
motivation theory the exam draws on, plus when specific team-building activities happen.

## Leadership styles

**Situational** is the most common/default answer — matching style to the specific situation rather
than applying one style universally. Others to recognize: Servant, Democratic, Transformational,
Laissez-faire, Transactional, Charismatic, Authentic, Autocratic, and Interactional (a mix of
Charismatic, Transactional, Transformational). Servant leadership (coaching, mentoring, removing
impediments) is generally preferred in modern/agile contexts — see the seeded glossary term.

## Power types

Legitimate (Formal/Positional), Reward, Penalty (Coercive), Referent, Expert, Situational,
Informational, Ingratiating, Guilt-based, Pressure-based, Persuasive, Relational. The exam tests
recognizing which type of power a scenario is exercising:

| Power type | Source of the power |
|---|---|
| Legitimate / Formal / Positional | The official position someone holds (e.g. being assigned PM) |
| Reward | Ability to compensate with monetary or non-monetary rewards (recognition, promotion, training) |
| Penalty / Coercive | Ability to penalize — uses fear of negative consequences |
| Referent | Being respected and admired for one's credibility |
| Expert | Deep skills and long experience in a specific field |
| Situational | Being the only one able to step up and rescue the team/project from a crisis |
| Informational | Controlling/owning the gathering and distribution of data |
| Ingratiating | Using flattery to find common ground and gain cooperation |
| Guilt-based | Making people feel guilty (e.g. "not living up to expectations") to gain compliance |
| Pressure-based | Limiting freedom of choice to force compliance |
| Persuasive | Convincing others through arguments |
| Relational | Built through a network of connections and alliances |

## Motivation theories

- **Theory X/Y/Z:** X assumes people dislike work and need control; Y assumes people are self-motivated
  and seek responsibility; Z (Ouchi/Motivational Theory Z) emphasizes long-term employment, commitment,
  opportunity, and advancement — performance is tied to being challenged and motivated
  (Japanese-management-influenced).
- **Expectancy Theory:** motivation depends on the belief that effort leads to performance, and
  performance leads to a valued reward. If the expected reward doesn't materialize, motivation drops.
- **Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory:** distinguishes intrinsic motivators (achievement, recognition,
  growth — a.k.a. Motivators/Motivating agents) from extrinsic hygiene factors (salary, conditions,
  policies — a.k.a. Demotivators/Hygiene agents) — hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction but don't
  themselves motivate.
- **Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs:** five levels, satisfied bottom-up — Physiological (air, food, sleep),
  Safety (security, predictability), Love & Belonging (friendship, intimacy), Esteem (recognition,
  status, respect), Self-actualization (fully realizing personal potential). A higher level only
  becomes a motivator once the levels below it are fulfilled — e.g. a team member worried about job
  security (Safety) won't be motivated by an Esteem-level reward like public recognition.

## MBTI (Myers-Briggs Type Indicator)

Four preference pairs the exam may ask you to infer from a scenario or name directly:

| Preference | Traits |
|---|---|
| Extrovert (E) vs. Introvert (I) | E: energized by people, quick pace, good at multitasking. I: prefers working alone/small groups, deliberate pace, one task at a time |
| Sensor (S) vs. Intuitive (N) | S: realistic, focuses on facts/details, applies common sense/past experience. N: focuses on possibilities/big picture, sees patterns, values innovation |
| Thinker (T) vs. Feeler (F) | T: decides via logical analysis, weighs pros/cons objectively, values consistency/fairness. F: decides via personal values and impact on others, sensitive and cooperative |
| Judger (J) vs. Perceiver (P) | J: organized, makes and sticks to plans, comfortable with rules. P: keeps opinions open, acts spontaneously, flexible with plans |

A team member who volunteers to mentor/coach others (Extrovert) but pushes their own solutions in
meetings without weighing alternatives (Judger) is a classic combined-trait exam scenario.

## Team development and when things happen

Tuckman's stages — forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning — pair with specific PM actions:

- The **team charter** (goals, roles, ground rules, and expectations) is established during
  **Forming**, at the outset — it sets the foundation for how the team will work together through the
  rest of the stages, rather than waiting for norms to emerge organically.
- **Team-building** activities are used during **Storming**, when conflict is most likely, as
  different personalities emerge and can cause conflicts/poor performance.
- **Drexler-Sibbet Team Performance Model** is an alternative (non-Tuckman) 7-step team performance
  model: Orientation, Trust building, Goal clarification, Commitment, Implementation, High
  performance, Renewal.

If the team is confused about goals/roles: review project goals, roles, and responsibilities with
them. If a team member has a performance issue, work it as a diagnostic sequence, not a single fix:
recognize the symptoms → reach out and talk to the person to find the actual cause → offer help →
monitor/measure progress → share feedback. The underlying cause matters — e.g. a senior performer
losing motivation because the work feels beneath them needs *more challenging work* (an intrinsic-
motivation fix), not a raise or team-building activity. Publicly discussing an individual's poor
performance with the whole team, or a competitive bonus system that pits underperformers against top
performers, are not the answer. Hiring an external replacement is not the favored fix, and
firing/replacing/releasing a team member is generally the wrong answer on the exam unless the scenario
clearly calls for it — it should be the last resort after coaching/support options are exhausted.

If a *new* team member is stuck making no progress on a task: pair them with a more experienced team
member to help them learn and overcome the impediment (rather than reassigning the task away from
them entirely, or just expressing disappointment).

If team members expect the PM to assign all their work despite a transition to Agile/self-organization:
**mentor them on how to make their own decisions** — this is what actually builds the capability to
self-organize, rather than just ensuring diverse functional expertise or removing impediments (both
useful, but don't address the root cause).

**Empowerment** — letting team members participate in decisions and own their actions — improves team
productivity and outcomes. **Emotional intelligence** (see glossary) is specifically about
understanding and sympathizing with others' emotions, not just managing your own.

## Team-building techniques

- **Pairing / Pair work:** two team members work simultaneously on the same work item.
- **Mobbing:** multiple team members focus simultaneously and coordinate contributions on one work
  item.
- **Swarming:** multiple team members focus collectively on resolving a specific impediment.
- **Shadowing:** a team member follows an expert/professional for observation, to learn a job/role.
- **Reverse shadowing:** a junior member is shadowed *by* a senior colleague, so the senior can learn
  the junior's perspective/skills.

## Remote/virtual teams

Remote/virtual teams are the norm now; the PM and team lean on online collaboration tools to stay
connected and address communication challenges specific to distribution:

- **Fishbowl windows:** an ongoing, long-lived video-conferencing feed between locations (join at the
  start of the workday, leave at the end) so a distributed team feels/acts as if co-located.
- **Remote pairing:** two or more people join an online event at a specific time to discuss and share
  screens as needed (Agile Practice Guide, p. 46).
- **Daily stand-ups** build connection and reduce rework even across a geographically/culturally
  distributed team where true co-location isn't feasible — a better fix than email-only communication
  (which raises misinterpretation risk) or cultural-difference training alone (which doesn't sync the
  actual work).
- **Time zone conflicts** (e.g. a stand-up time that doesn't suit everyone) are resolved by
  **compromise** — since the constraint is outside anyone's control, the goal is some degree of
  satisfaction for all parties, not a perfect outcome. See
  [conflict-resolution.md](conflict-resolution.md).
