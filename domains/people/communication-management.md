# Communication Management

Covers the People domain ECO task: plan and manage communication (and supports help ensure knowledge
transfer).

## Communication strategy vs. Communication Management Plan

The exam distinguishes a high-level strategy from the tactical plan that implements it — don't treat
them as synonyms:

- **Communication strategy:** the structured, high-level blueprint for managing information flow on
  the project. It defines the overall approach to engaging stakeholders so communication is
  purposeful, consistent, and aligned with the project's vision and constraints.
- **Communication Management Plan** (a.k.a. Communication Plan): the detailed, tactical document that
  actually specifies the methods, timelines, and tools used to get the right information to the right
  stakeholders at the right time. It's what a PM works from day to day.

## The three core questions of the Communication Management Plan: Who, What, How

- **Who:** the target audience — key stakeholders, executives (e.g. the CEO), and other stakeholder
  groups — along with their expectations and interests. Pulls directly from stakeholder
  identification/analysis; see [stakeholder-engagement.md](stakeholder-engagement.md).
- **What:** the types of messages and the formats used to deliver them — status reports, dashboards,
  spreadsheets, digital or paper-based updates.
- **How:** the delivery method and tools (email, meetings, etc.), the level of formality, and the
  frequency of updates (weekly, monthly, or as-needed).

A well-structured plan is what keeps communication actionable and consistent day to day — including
during quiet stretches when project status isn't changing, updates still need to go out on schedule
rather than lapsing.

## The communication model

The basic sender/receiver model has five components: **sender → message → channel → receiver →
feedback**. **Noise** is anything that interferes with the message getting through:

- **Physiological noise:** talking too fast/slow, or other speaker-side physical factors.
- **Psychological noise:** judgment, sarcasm, bias.
- **Physical noise:** a literal environmental disruption (a baby crying, background noise).
- **Semantic noise:** jargon or word choice the receiver doesn't understand.

## Delivery style: push, pull, and interactive

- **Push communication:** the sender sends information out without an active request from the
  receiver — email, newsletters, memos, automated notifications. Fine for routine updates, but not
  guaranteed to be read or absorbed.
- **Pull communication:** the sender publishes content the receiver accesses on their own —
  wikis/handbooks, shared drives, dashboards the PMIS exposes. This shifts the effort of seeking
  information onto the receiver, which means it may not be sufficient on its own for high-power,
  demanding stakeholders who expect information to come to them.
- **Interactive / synchronous communication:** real-time back-and-forth — meetings, calls,
  videoconferencing. The richest channel, but the most expensive in everyone's time; reserve it for
  complex or high-power/high-interest situations, layering push and pull underneath it rather than
  replacing it.
- **Asynchronous communication:** the counterpart to interactive — parties aren't present at the same
  time (email, async messaging) and don't expect an instant reply.

A demanding, high-power sponsor on a complex project generally needs *more* than a monthly pull-only
setup — frequent (weekly/bi-weekly) touchpoints plus access to real-time detail (e.g. standups) beats
either a single comprehensive report or bare dashboard access alone.

## Communication flow and formality

- **Flow direction:** downward (manager → team), upward (team → manager), horizontal (peer ↔ peer,
  same level), diagonal (across levels and functions), external (outside the project/org). Vertical
  communication spans hierarchical levels (e.g. PM ↔ CEO) — it's the general case that downward/upward
  are specific directions of.
- **Formal communication:** structured, follows predetermined channels — official meetings, reports,
  documented exchanges, carefully planned.
- **Informal communication:** casual and spontaneous — hallway conversations, chats, ad hoc calls.
- **Osmotic communication:** absorbing information subconsciously by overhearing colocated teammates'
  discussions — an argument for colocation (see the seeded Colocation glossary term).

## Mediums, methods, channels — three overlapping words, three different meanings

- **Communication methods:** the broad category of *how* — verbal, non-verbal, listening, written,
  visual.
- **Communication mediums:** the physical/technical means info travels through — voice, writing, body
  language, or mass media (TV, internet).
- **Communication channels:** the *possible connections* between parties — this is what the
  n(n-1)/2 formula counts (see below), not a method or medium.

## Communication style and written communication quality

- **Communication style:** passive, aggressive, passive-aggressive, or assertive. Assertive —
  direct and honest without intentionally hurting anyone's feelings — is generally the target style
  for a PM.
- **5Cs of written communication:** effective written communication should be Correct, Concise, Clear,
  Coherent, and Controlled.
- **Nonverbal communication:** non-linguistic signals (facial expression, gesture, space, touch, eye
  contact, tone). **Paralingual communication** is the voice-specific subset — tone, volume, pitch,
  intonation.

## Interpersonal skills and group techniques

- **Interpersonal skills:** active listening, empathy, conflict resolution, teamwork, effective
  communication, relationship building — the skill set that makes all of the above actually work in
  practice.
- **Round-Robin:** going around a group so each person shares input in turn — ensures everyone
  contributes and no one dominates the discussion.
- **Storytelling:** a narrative technique using telling and listening to convey information.

## Communication channels formula

For a team of `n` people, the number of potential communication channels is:

**n(n − 1) / 2**

Used to illustrate why communication complexity grows fast as a team scales — adding people increases
channel count quadratically, not linearly. A classic exam formula question: given a team size (before
and after adding members), compute the channel count or the increase in channels.

## Active listening

The receiving half of communication — not just how a message is sent (Communication Management Plan),
but how a PM actually takes one in. Seven principles:

1. **Allow time to speak.** Don't rush to reply before the other person finishes — patience shows
   respect and ensures you actually grasp their point before responding.
2. **Body language and eye contact.** Non-verbal cues (eye contact, nodding, brief acknowledgments)
   signal engagement and create an attentive environment.
3. **Ask questions.** Clarify and deepen understanding rather than assuming — keeps the conversation
   constructive and confirms both parties agree on what was said.
4. **Paraphrase and clarify.** Reword what the speaker said back to them — confirms your understanding
   and gives them a chance to correct any misconception before it compounds.
5. **Avoid distractions.** Phone-checking or mental preoccupation blocks real understanding — remove
   distractions to create space for meaningful communication.
6. **Avoid judgment.** Preconceived notions and biases distort the message — focus on understanding
   the speaker's perspective before evaluating it.
7. **Summarize and move forward.** Close a conversation by summarizing key points and agreeing on next
   steps — this is what turns a discussion into a tangible outcome, not just a chat.

On the exam, active listening shows up in scenario questions about handling disagreement, resolving
conflict (see [conflict-resolution.md](conflict-resolution.md)), and stakeholder conversations — the
"right" answer is often whichever option reflects genuinely listening and confirming understanding
before acting, rather than jumping straight to a decision or an escalation.

## Cultural sensitivity and cross-border communication

When stakeholder feedback flags a PM's communication style as too harsh/abrasive (common in
cross-cultural, remote-stakeholder scenarios), the right move is to acknowledge the impact and adjust
tone going forward — not to defend the style as "clear and direct," and not to just switch delivery
medium without addressing the actual tone problem. **Cultural awareness/sensitivity** means recognizing
these differences proactively, before they cause friction — e.g. validating each stakeholder's
understanding live during a global kickoff, rather than assuming a single communication style works
everywhere.

## Remote and asynchronous meeting comprehension

If team members forget, miss, or don't fully understand what happened in a remote meeting: add a group
norm for people to flag when they don't understand something in the moment, *and* record sessions so
anyone can refer back later. Both together — not just switching meeting frequency, and not asking
people to change how they naturally speak.

## Other terms worth knowing

- **Buy-in:** the result of reaching consensus or alignment with a proposition, solution, or plan.
- **Feedback loops:** the ongoing process of collecting, analyzing, and implementing feedback.
  **Feedback mechanisms** are the structures set up to enable it.
- **Communication flow chart:** a graphical map of how information flows through an org/project —
  process, departments, individuals, and channels.
- **Communication metrics:** KPIs for communication itself — engagement rate, open rate, page visits,
  satisfaction rate.
- **Influence Diagram:** a model showing how a project's events/activities influence each other.
- **Status meeting:** a regularly scheduled event to exchange and analyze current project
  progress/performance information.

## Knowledge transfer

- **Explicit knowledge:** easily codified and shared via documentation (procedures, guides, wikis).
- **Tacit knowledge:** personal, experience-based knowledge that's hard to write down — shared through
  mentoring, pairing, communities of practice, and direct conversation rather than documents.

Ensuring knowledge transfer is its own ECO task, distinct from general communication planning — it's
specifically about not losing know-how (especially tacit) as people or phases roll off the project.
